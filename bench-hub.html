    Frame — skeletal, open back

    The newer design: the panel is deleted, the DIN rail is the structure, the rear tray carries storage and stiffness. Everything below the modules is open on purpose.

    Console — moulded, solid back

    The earlier philosophy: one big print with wells and bins moulded in, and a solid raked rear panel the rail screws to — the white wall in the rear view.

    Display mount — v1 H-frame vs v2 posts

    v2 is now what the frame ships. v1 was spine + two uprights + two crossbars: 5 parts, 63.4 cm³, ~81 g, plus eight M3 × 12 that existed only to bolt the mount to itself. v2 is one part printed twice — a T-slot post with its own clip — spaced on the rail to the display’s holes: 2 parts, 25.1 cm³, ~32 g. 60% less plastic. The uprights only ever needed a spine because their spacing was fixed; the rail already gives X and each post’s slot gives Y, so nothing is left that exists just to hold other parts. v1 is still selectable in the 3D viewer as Mount v1.

    Breadboard retention — five concepts

    Nothing stops a board sliding along the 288 mm cradle today. Each concept below is staged on the real deck at the 830/400 boundary. Stops in orange.

    Board retention — the cleats, measured and drawn

    The printed teeth are gone. Retention now uses the board’s own dovetail cleats: a pocket on one rail takes the male tab, a peg on the other drops into the notch opposite, and the board goes straight down onto both. The first two pictures are the dimensions themselves — every number drawn against the feature it spans, because five separate attempts to measure the cleat spacing disagreed and “the spacing” was never pinned to a specific pair of faces.

    Joint study

    The question under review: the end frame’s rail joint reads as a thick block patched behind the mortise, and its gasket pocket (the indent) may be redundant now the shroud captures the rail by shape. Each candidate below was built as real geometry by an independent agent, then attacked by two more (structural + printability). Angles: front three-quarter, rear-top (boss depth and gasket land), inner-face silhouette. Click any image to zoom.

    Tie-rod joint · second pass · no brackets

    Where the tray meets the rear tie

    The first pass asked “what bracket holds the rod?” and got five trapezoids for an answer. Wrong question. The actual offence is that TRAY_Y1 sits at TIE_Y − TIE_R − 2, so there is a 2 mm slot running the whole 288 mm between the tray's rear wall and the rod. That slot is the gap — and the tails were lumps stuck on to bridge it.

    So: delete both tails and let the rear wall do the job along its entire length. The wall comes down and lands on the rod, and the rod's own cutter carves the mating surface, so the fit is conforming by construction and there is nothing to align. Contact goes from 21 mm² to ~1400 mm² — and the part count goes down, not up.

    How far back the wall may come is not a style choice. The tray prints upright, so the wall's underside may not exceed 45°. That lands it exactly on the rod's 45° tangent point, y = 144.65 — the geometry hands over the design line for free. The three options differ only in how the wall face gets there.

    Buttress tops · a printability study, measured

    Two seconds a layer, 190 layers running

    The tray is 288 × 53 × 53 and 61 g. Below z = 14 it prints 1 300–14 000 mm² layers. Above the rim it collapses to two isolated 6 × 25 mm fins and stays there for ~190 layers — 4.8 s at the rim, 2.3 s at the rail land. Nothing cools before the next layer lands, and it happens on the one surface that has to be accurate.

    Geometry can only do so much. Layer time = area × height ÷ flow, so 8 s at 0.2 mm and 12 mm³/s needs ~480 mm² — more than 3× what the fins give, and the H2S runs faster than 12, which makes every number here optimistic. All times below are measured off the real solids at 0.2 mm / 12 mm³/s.

    First pass — five brackets, rejected

    Kept for the record and for one genuinely useful finding: the tie's flat sits on the desk plane, so nothing can reach under it. No closed eye, no hook below centreline, no split bearing — the build proved that last one by handing back a U-block whose two cheeks were separate solids with nothing to join them. Every bracket could only ever work the top 180°, and every one of them read as a trapezoid bolted to the back.
